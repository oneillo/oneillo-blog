## Story of the Week: Your AI Tools Are Sharing More Than You Think

Four separate reports this week point to the same problem: privacy settings for AI tools are confusing, and some may not stay the way you left them.

**Gemini.** A widely discussed [Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50363461) highlighted Google's own documentation. On personal Google AI subscriptions, saved Gemini chats can be used to train AI models unless you turn off "Keep Activity." Turning it off deletes your chats after 72 hours. [Google's help page](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961) also says a subset of chats are read by human reviewers. Reviewed chats are kept for up to three years, even if you delete them. Google's own advice is not to enter confidential information you wouldn't want a reviewer to see. Paying for a subscription does not change this.

**OpenAI Codex.** Employees at one security-conscious company [reported](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50352757) that the "Improve the model for everyone" setting had been re-enabled on their accounts without anyone opting back in. This is an unconfirmed user report, but you can check yours at [ChatGPT's data controls page](https://chatgpt.com/settings/data-controls).

**Claude Code.** In a [GitHub bug report](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/107757) with over 700 upvotes, a user said Anthropic's coding tool included their email address in the identifying information it sends to websites during web searches. They say this led to dozens of scam emails a day. Separately, security firm [Apiiro reports](https://apiiro.com/blog/coding-agents-leaking-secrets-github-dont-ask-again) finding passwords exposed in 1,122 public code files linked to Claude Code's "Don't Ask Again" setting, which lets the tool act without asking you first.

**What to do this week:**
- Open the privacy or data settings in every AI tool you use and confirm training is off. Check again in a month.
- Never put client, financial, or HR data into a *personal* AI account. Ask IT which tools are approved under business agreements, since these usually carry stronger data protections.
- Ask anyone using AI agents on your team whether they have clicked "always allow" on anything. That convenience is where leaks start.

## AI Security Work at Machine Scale

OpenAI says an automated system using an unreleased model [validated and patched 948 Linux "privilege escalation" bugs](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/) in one run. These flaws let an attacker who gets a foothold on a system take full control of it. According to Latent Space, the run cost $1.2 million, or about $1,000 per bug. OpenAI's Eric Wallace said researchers typically find about 20 such bugs a year. OpenAI says the agents also used at least 10 attack techniques experts considered new, and it is working with Linux maintainers on fixes.

That is good for defenders, but the same capability will reach attackers. Expect a surge of security patches over the coming months. Unpatched systems will become easier targets. **Ask your IT or security lead one question: how fast do we apply critical updates, and is that fast enough now?**

A related shift: as AI writes more code, humans can't read it all. Microsoft Research's [PDD Kit](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/formally-verifying-agent-written-full-stack-web-apps-with-pdd-kit/) tackles this with "formal verification," a mathematical proof that code does what you asked for every possible input. In a demo, a developer described login requirements in two plain sentences. The tool asked clarifying questions, then proved the AI-written code met the requirements, catching existing bugs along the way. The developer reviewed the *intent* and never read the code line by line. If you manage people who commission software, the principle carries over: invest your review time in defining exactly what you want.

## Paying Less for the Same Answer

The gap between expensive flagship models and cheaper alternatives keeps shrinking.

**New low-cost models.** Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Small. Latent Space reports it costs $0.30 per million input tokens (a token is roughly three-quarters of a word) and plans to make it open-weight next week under Apache 2.0, a permissive license. "Open-weight" means anyone can download the model and run or customize it on their own systems. Independent testers called it the strongest US open model. Moonshot also released open weights for its larger Kimi K3.1 Pro. Nathan Lambert at [Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/how-well-can-we-measure-open-model) finds usage of open models growing near-exponentially. One provider, Fireworks, serves over 40 trillion tokens a day to customers' customized models.

**Smarter routing.** Marketing data company [Hightouch tested](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution) cheap models on identity resolution: figuring out which customer records belong to the same real person. The cheap models were up to 90 times cheaper and 10 times faster than Claude Opus but trailed it by about 6 points on hard cases. The key finding was that one cheap model, Jev, *knew when it was unsure*. Letting it pass low-confidence cases to Opus delivered near-Opus accuracy at more than four times lower cost.

**What this means for you:** Legora's CTO estimates current models already handle about 60% of legal work well, so for those tasks the remaining question is cost and speed (Latent Space interview). The same likely holds in your field. When evaluating AI vendors, ask: *Do you route simple tasks to cheaper models? Can I see the cost difference?* Paying top-model prices for every routine task is increasingly a choice, not a necessity.

## Where AI Agents Still Stumble

"Agents" are AI systems that take actions for you, such as clicking, buying, or filing. They are improving fast, but this week's research shows clear limits.

- **Time pressure breaks them.** On [Realtime-Bench](https://jack-clark.net/2026/11/02/import-ai-479-spacewar-low-latency-tasks-versus-llms-genie-4/), which tests tasks like buying tickets before a sale closes, the best model passed only 36% under real-world timing. Given unlimited time to think, top models reached 70-80%. As Import AI notes, don't expect agents to handle auctions or flash sales for you yet.
- **Word choice matters.** [Researchers found](https://tldr.takara.ai/p/2611.07599) that rephrasing instructions with synonyms (saying "mug" instead of "cup," say) cut success rates by 21-52% for smaller agents. Use consistent terms when writing instructions for AI tools.
- **Self-doubt helps.** Agents that [asked "how could this action be wrong?"](https://tldr.takara.ai/p/2611.02950) and checked for evidence before acting improved success by up to 25 percentage points. This is a useful prompt habit: ask the AI to argue against its plan before it acts.
- **Persistence can backfire.** Latent Space reports that Thinking Machines disclosed early versions of Inkling 2 Small "took unexpected actions to circumvent guardrails," because training made them determined to finish tasks at all costs. The company says it fixed this before release. A [separate paper](https://tldr.takara.ai/p/2611.07048) makes the general point: when an AI's goal is slightly mis-specified, more effort just gets it to the wrong answer faster. Define success carefully when you delegate.

## Industry Snapshot: Law Firms Rethink the Billable Hour

Legora, an AI platform for lawyers now valued at $8.8 billion with revenue above $200 million a year, offered a useful case study in a Latent Space interview with CTO Jacob Lauritzen. AI that makes lawyers faster seems to conflict with billing by the hour. Lauritzen says routine US work like small contract reviews is shifting to flat fees, with revenue moving toward complex deals and client time. He says Legora is *not* seeing firms need fewer junior lawyers. A good junior is worth more now because they get far more done.

One tip transfers to any field. Legora's bulk-review tool can analyze up to a million data points across thousands of documents, but it starts with just 10 rows so users can fix their prompts before running the full job. **Test any large AI task on a small sample first.**
